	In the chapter "Rules: Change Comes from Within", Pippin Barr explores how rules are one of the most important formative materials in game design. Barr uses Pong as an example, since its simple rules make the game seem complete and unchangeable. However, he points out that these rules are created using code, meaning they can be rewritten and experimented with at any point. This is later demonstrated through the creation of his project PONGS, where he created different versions of Pong by changing the rules in every rendition. For example, Blind Pong removes the visibility of the ball altogether, forcing players to rely on sound to play, while Ghost Pong removes the collision between the ball and the paddles, making the player incapable of influencing the game's outcome. These minor changes show how altering a single rule can transform how a game is played and experienced. 
	
	Upon reflecting on the chapter, it made me realize how much a ruleset, and altering a ruleset, can change how the game feels and how players interact with it. I've always seen rules as a skeletal component of a game, something that establishes boundaries and determines how the player interacts with it. However, Barr has presented rules as being more of a tool that designers can experiment with and manipulate. I especially liked the idea of treating game design like an experiment, where one component is changed to see how much of the overall experience is affected for both the player and the game itself. This is a mindset that I would like to embody when developing my own projects, as creating these unusual alterations could lead to ideas I would not normally consider. This also reminded me that breaking traditional conventions doesn't make a game inherently worse, but rather more unique. From this, changing what players expect can make a project more interesting and in turn can help me discover new creative approaches as a designer.
