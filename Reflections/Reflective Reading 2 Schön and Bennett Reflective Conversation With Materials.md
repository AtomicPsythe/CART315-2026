	"Reflective Conversation with Materials" is an interview conducted by John Bennett with Donald Schön who discusses how the process of design is not a straightforward one, but rather one that is constantly in motion. Instead of following the preassumed process of creating an idea and translating it into a finished product, Schön believes that designers grow by interacting with what they are creating and learn from their unexpected mistakes, to which I symapthize with. Unexpected mistakes, or happy accidents, happen the most when I'm in the early development stages of a project, where the concept isn't fully concrete. This can be related to the idea of "reflection in action", as it describes how designers notice and learn from their mistakes by adapting their process should they decide to move forward with it. Schön calls this a "conversation with materials", where the results of our actions, our creations, "talk back" to us.

	This can also be related to my experience with the recent Pong prototyping assignments. For instance, a change I had wanted to implement was to have the ball's velocity increase every time it interacted with the paddle. I struggled with making it work for hours, but it resulted in something better. Instead of the velocity purely increasing, it had a chance to decrease the ball's velocity, impacting the tempo of the game in a way that didn't cross my mind. I found the result very fascinating and ultimately decided to keep it, since it complimented my later change of spawning new balls nicely. As a result, this connects to Schön's argument that there is no linear path for design, as every design decision can lead to numerous unintended consequences. 

	Upon reflecting on Schön's idea of of our creations "talk back" to us, it made me feel very paternal about my decisions, as they get it personified in a way that forces me to put more thought and care into them. From this, the process of prototyping so far has taught me that testing is an important part of the process, but it's not just about testing whether an idea works. It is also about teaching me what the idea is, how it may need to be changed, and if it adequately translated to ohters. This naturally can bring about some frustrating moments, like when code breaks, but it more often than not leads to more fruitful results in the end.
